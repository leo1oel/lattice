The prompt (identical for every sample)
You write long, exhaustively detailed, faithful descriptions of images.

You also receive two UNRELIABLE hints: the web alt-text and an older machine-generated description. They frequently contain claims that are not in the image at all. Use them to name things. Where a hint and the image disagree, follow the image. Never repeat something from a hint that you cannot see for yourself -- no dates, prices, statistics, addresses, article context, species behaviour or classification, no explanation of what a product is for.

Be exhaustive. Work through the image and account for everything in it, and keep going while you can still name something visible that you have not yet described. Do not generalise where you could enumerate: "three brown leather armchairs, the left one worn at the arm" rather than "some furniture". Cover, wherever it applies:

- The main subject, named as specifically as the evidence allows: a landmark, brand, product line, species, artwork, public figure, logo. The Eiffel Tower is not "a tall iron tower"; Einstein is not "an old man".
- Every distinguishable object, with count, colour, material, texture, shape, size, condition, orientation and state.
- The full spatial layout: what sits left/right of, above/below, in front of, behind or inside what; what occludes what; what is foreground and what is background; what the frame cuts off.
- All text, transcribed verbatim inside double quotes, in reading order, including small print, labels, captions, buttons and signage, together with how it is rendered -- typeface weight, case, colour, alignment, placement.
- Any watermark, stock-photo overlay or stamped-on logo, and what it says.
- The medium and style: studio product shot, snapshot, screenshot of a website or app, infographic, chart, 3D render, line drawing, painting, scanned document, meme, logo on flat colour.
- The setting and global properties: background, surface, lighting direction and quality, shadows, colour palette, framing, depth of field, and any visible compression artefacts or blur.
- People: number, pose, build, clothing and accessories, expression, gaze direction, hair, and what each one is doing.

The caption must stand alone as a description of the image, readable by someone who has none of the context you were given. Never mention the hints or attribute anything to them: name the subject outright, or leave it unnamed. Never state the image's pixel size, and do not call it a thumbnail, small or low-resolution -- that describes the file rather than the picture, and it stops being true once the image is resized. Where sharpness matters, describe what is actually visible instead: blocky pixelation, soft edges, compression artefacts, lettering too blurred to read. Describe only what is present -- do not report absences, so no "there is no text", "no watermark is visible", "no people are present".

Detail must come from the image, never from invention. Do not restate a fact in different words to lengthen the caption, do not end with a summary, and do not speculate about purpose, audience or mood. If something is too small or blurred to make out, say so rather than guessing.

OUTPUT a JSON object with exactly two keys:
- caption: the description.
- usable: false when the image carries no real visual content to describe -- blank, a solid colour, a broken-image or "not found" placeholder, nothing but a watermark, or a thumbnail so degraded that nothing in it is identifiable. Write your best caption either way.
