# Revision and scientific style

Revise from meaning outward. The final prose should sound like a researcher who
knows exactly what the evidence says, not a model performing the genre of a
paper.

## Revision order

Run separate passes. Do not combine factual correction with stylistic rewriting.

1. **Provenance**: facts, numbers, citations, quotations, and derived values.
2. **Claims**: support, causality, scope, uncertainty, and counterevidence.
3. **Architecture**: reader questions, section roles, evidence order, and
   duplication.
4. **Alignment**: title, abstract, introduction, results, figures, limitations,
   and conclusion.
5. **Terminology**: names, notation, metrics, populations, units, and comparison
   direction.
6. **Paragraphs and sentences**: information flow, emphasis, and clarity.
7. **Generic model prose**: formulaic rhetoric, filler, and metronomic cadence.
8. **Venue style**: length, headings, citations, typography, and LaTeX.

After any style pass, rerun the provenance and number checks.

## Author-draft-first revision

When revising the researcher's own target draft, preserve provenance as well as
meaning. Hash the input before editing. Replace protected material—numbers,
units, citations, quotations, equations, code, tables, figure references, and
named entities—with typed placeholders during any broad prose operation, then
restore and verify it exactly.

Prefer a local edit to a paragraph rewrite and a paragraph rewrite to a section
rewrite. A fluent replacement is not an improvement when the original already
states the evidence clearly. Preserve unusual but valid sentence movement,
punctuation, first-person use, and paragraph extent rather than normalizing them
to a generic house voice.

After revision, audit in both directions:

- for each source claim, locate its revised expression and compare polarity,
  modality, scope, comparator, and evidence;
- for each revised factual statement, locate its source in the author draft or
  research record;
- compare all entities, quantities, citations, equations, and limitations;
- list generated passages and unresolved changes instead of hiding them.

An author style model, when authorized, should be built from a substantial
same-author, same-genre corpus and held out by document. Use aggregate,
section-conditioned characteristics such as information order, stance,
paragraph jobs, and syntax. Do not store preferred phrases, sentence templates,
lexical bans, or punctuation targets, and reject non-required phrase overlap
with the corpus.

## Reader-expectation pass

Readers use structure to decide what matters. At each scale:

- begin with the prior context needed to understand what follows;
- put the new or emphatic information where the sentence or paragraph resolves;
- keep a grammatical subject close to its verb when intervening material would
  obscure the action;
- put the action in the verb rather than hiding it in an abstract noun;
- make each paragraph perform one identifiable argumentative job;
- state logical links that are obvious to the author but absent from the text.

These are principles, not a sentence generator. Passive voice is useful when
the object or procedure, rather than the actor, is the continuing topic. Long
sentences are acceptable when their clauses resolve cleanly. Do not regularize
all prose into the same length.

## Paragraph pass

For each paragraph, write a margin note naming its job. Examples include define
the problem, establish a control, report a result, reject an alternative, or
narrow a claim. If the job is merely "transition" or "background," ask whether
the paragraph carries enough information to remain.

Check that:

- the opening connects to established context;
- the paragraph advances one reader question;
- swapping it with a neighbor would damage the argument; otherwise make the
  dependency explicit or combine the independent material by purpose;
- abstractions are followed by an instance when an instance is available;
- evidence appears before the strongest verdict it supports;
- the final emphasis lands on information, not ceremony;
- adjacent paragraphs do not repeat the same claim in new words;
- paragraph lengths follow the material rather than a uniform template.

## Sentence pass

- Prefer concrete subjects and verbs.
- Name the actor behind a claim: cite who argues, observes, or assumes.
- Replace an unscoped adjective with a measurement or condition. "Scalable"
  needs the tested resource range; "efficient" needs a cost comparison.
- Give every comparison an object, metric, population, and direction.
- Use one uncertainty marker with specific content. State what is unknown, the
  live alternatives, and what evidence would distinguish them.
- Define a technical term once and repeat it consistently. Synonym cycling
  harms technical writing.
- Use pronouns only when their referent is unmistakable. "This result" is often
  clearer than a bare "this."
- Break a sentence when it asks one stress position to carry multiple unrelated
  conclusions, not merely because it crosses a word count.
- Read equations as part of the sentence and define symbols near first use.

## Remove generic model prose

Look for clusters, not isolated words or punctuation. Revise the underlying
writing problem instead of swapping vocabulary mechanically.

### Keep the evidence hierarchy implicit

Private labels such as explanatory, decisive, corroborating, supporting, and
boundary evidence help allocate attention; they are rarely reader-facing
claims. Let hierarchy emerge from order, space, exact values, and the questions
each experiment resolves. Do not repeatedly announce that a comparison is
"central," "decisive," or of a particular "inferential weight."

Similarly, do not turn the Introduction into an inventory of experiment classes
or the Discussion into a certification report. Establish the scientific
distinction, develop the measurements that answer it, and reason to a small
number of non-obvious consequences. Keep an epistemic qualification where it
changes the claim. Otherwise put the tested condition inside the claim and move
on.

### Empty framing

Cut openings that announce the importance, rapid growth, complexity, or
transformative potential of a field without identifying the paper's problem.
Start with the actual observation, constraint, question, or contribution.

### Inflated significance

Replace "pivotal," "groundbreaking," "crucial," "robust," "novel," and similar
evaluations with the property, comparison, or evidence meant by the word. Keep
a term when it has a defined technical meaning. Turn broad forecasts into a
testable prediction with stated conditions, or mark them as conjecture.

### Vague attribution

Replace "researchers believe," "studies show," and "it is widely known" with a
real source and precise claim. If no source exists in the packet, remove the
claim or expose the gap.

### Throat-clearing and announcements

Phrases such as "it is important to note," "in this section we," and "taken
together" often delay a point already clear from the structure. State the
point. Keep signposting when a dense argument genuinely needs orientation.

Remove leaked assistant scaffolding such as "Certainly," "Here is the revised
section," or offers to help further when it has entered manuscript prose. Keep
explicit research placeholders visible until the author resolves them; never
turn a placeholder into a plausible fact merely to make the draft read cleanly.

### Manufactured rhetoric

Watch for repeated forms such as:

- "not only X but Y" and "it is not X; it is Y";
- question-then-immediate-answer hooks;
- stacks of short fragments written as punchlines;
- forced groups of three;
- every section ending with the same sweeping takeaway;
- a metaphor that never becomes a precise scientific distinction.

One instance may be apt. Repetition reveals a template. Rewrite around the
research logic rather than banning the form.

Keep mirrored syntax for a real side-by-side comparison. Do not make every
scope limit another mirrored slogan such as "X, not Y." State the positive
evidentiary limit directly when no comparison needs emphasis.

### Synthetic completeness

Generated paragraphs often try to be locally exhaustive. A compact unit may
define the whole problem, give one matched clause to every side of a
distinction, cover each task or metric, state the method, and close with a
balanced qualification. Every sentence can be correct while the paragraph
still reads as if it was expanded from a checklist rather than written to make
one scientific decision intelligible.

Look for a cluster of these symptoms:

- an abstract opening states the entire conceptual distinction before the
  reader encounters the behavior, result, or object that makes it necessary;
- concepts receive matched clauses because they are adjacent in the research
  record, even though one matters more to the paragraph's inference;
- every sentence combines observation, interpretation, and qualification, so
  none is allowed simply to report a result or explain a mechanism;
- transitions such as "whereas," "therefore," "in contrast," and "together"
  repeatedly announce relations that the neighboring facts already establish;
- the final sentence summarizes the paragraph instead of placing new evidence
  or a non-obvious consequence in the stress position;
- separate paragraphs repeat the same define-contrast-synthesize movement with
  different scientific nouns.

Repair the paragraph from its argumentative job, not by varying surface form:

1. Name the one question or decision the paragraph must resolve and the
   observation, failure, comparison, or derivation that creates pressure on it.
2. Enter at the most informative point permitted by prior context. This may be
   a concrete behavior, setup, value, or counterexample rather than a general
   definition.
3. Preserve real asymmetry. Give the result that changes the inference more
   space, and defer an adjacent task, metric, or caveat to the paragraph that
   owns it.
4. Let a sentence report, define, or reason without forcing it to perform all
   three jobs. Add an interpretation only when it advances beyond the stated
   evidence.
5. Do not manufacture a closing sentence. End on the strongest new information
   when no further inference is needed.

Deferral is not omission from the paper. Keep every conclusion-changing result
and qualification in its proper location, and keep a counterexample beside the
claim it reverses. The goal is selective development across paragraphs, not
incomplete reporting.

### Superficial analysis

Delete participial tails that merely announce significance: "..., highlighting
the importance of the result." Replace them with a supported consequence or
stop after the observation.

### Repetition and cadence

AI-shaped drafts often repeat paragraph openings, sentence frames, transition
words, and conclusion phrases while keeping every paragraph the same size.
Vary structure only where the reasoning varies. Do not add random short
sentences, asides, or errors to simulate a person.

Check argumentative cadence as well as sentence cadence. Results of unequal
importance should not all receive a same-sized paragraph that moves from
number, to interpretation, to disclaimer. Develop the decisive comparison with
its controls; attach a minor ablation to the claim it qualifies; end with a
boundary only when that boundary changes the result's interpretation.

Label each Results unit privately as `decisive comparison`, `control or
alternative`, or `boundary or cost`. Standalone prose development belongs to
the decisive comparison. Combine secondary evidence by the claim or alternative
it changes, while keeping exact values easy to locate. Keep every result in the
paper, but do not repeatedly promote a minor control across the abstract,
introduction, discussion, and conclusion. Do not preserve Results order in
Discussion unless that order itself forms the argument.

### Epistemic compression

Scientific caution should change the claim, not become a commentary track.
For each unresolved inference, keep one explicit statement that it is a
hypothesis and one sentence naming the experiment, intervention, proof, or
source that would distinguish the live alternatives. Elsewhere, state the
observation and alternative directly. Delete repeated announcements that a
result "supports," "is consistent with," or "does not prove" an interpretation
when an expert reader can already infer that status from the design. A later
qualification earns repetition only by adding a distinct consequence,
alternative explanation, or discriminating test; merely re-certifying the same
epistemic status stalls the argument.

When a positive claim is immediately followed by a generic negation or
universality disclaimer, first try putting the tested dataset, population,
architecture, metric, or condition inside the positive claim. Keep a separate
caveat when it reports a real counterexample, such as a failure at another
depth, or when combining it with the claim would hide the evidence.

### Abstract-introduction-conclusion echo

These sections cover related claims at different resolutions, but they should
not be lightly paraphrased copies. The abstract enables selection, the
introduction builds the problem and evidentiary promise, and the conclusion
states what survives after the evidence and limitations.

Assign each major boundary a primary location. Repeat it near another claim only
when it changes how that claim should be read; do not reproduce the complete
limitations inventory in the abstract, end of the introduction, Discussion,
and Conclusion merely to make each section look complete. Keep the single
boundary that most changes the headline result in the abstract.

Make a private boundary-allocation table before cutting repetitions. The final
Introduction paragraph states the research question and the evidence the paper
will use to answer it; it does not preview the full Results and Limitations
checklists. Preserve the relation among the concrete problem or question, the
central answer or contribution, and its decisive evidence; let the paper type
determine their exact order. Move the manifest of controls, ablations,
robustness checks, and costs to Methods or Results.

The manuscript should describe the research, not narrate assembly of an
evidence packet. After provenance is audited, state directly that an experiment
was run, a comparison is available, or a baseline is missing. Retain source or
provenance language when it changes validity, reproducibility, permissions, or
the distinction between author observations and external evidence.

After a result, add interpretation only when it advances to a non-obvious
inference. Do not report a measurement, paraphrase its direction, negate an
obvious misreading, and then restate the preferred interpretation. Explain a
metric or confound when the intended reader genuinely needs that bridge.

Discussion should not walk through Results in the same order with new verdict
verbs. Group findings by the inference they jointly support, the alternatives
they leave open, and the next test that would discriminate among them. The
Conclusion should then state the durable result and its decisive boundary,
rather than reciting every experiment and disclaimer once more.

## Preserve legitimate scientific prose

Do not flag or remove an expression merely because a detector might associate
it with generated text. Preserve:

- necessary technical terms and their repetition;
- conventional mathematical and statistical language;
- precise uncertainty and limitations;
- passive constructions that maintain the paragraph's topic;
- punctuation appropriate to the venue and sentence;
- parallel structure used for genuinely parallel concepts;
- an author's established voice when it remains clear and accurate.

Never evade a prose problem by changing a correct number, citation, claim,
technical term, or qualifier.

Do not optimize scientific prose for a detector score. In particular, do not
add typos, false starts, emotional punctuation, fabricated names or anecdotes,
or deliberate contradictions; do not drop supported findings to make the text
less orderly. Fixed word bans, punctuation quotas, and sentence-length targets
are not evidence of better writing. Use a suspected pattern only to locate a
reader or reasoning problem, then repair that problem while preserving the
research record. If the user asks for detector evasion, offer reader-facing
revision and factual auditing without promising or targeting a classifier
verdict.

## Voice calibration

When the author provides a sample, identify its register, sentence movement,
paragraph openings, punctuation, terminology, and degree of first-person use.
Match choices that are compatible with the venue and scientific record. Do not
copy unusual phrases from another researcher or imitate the personal voice of
a benchmark paper.

Without a sample, write plain professional English. Scientific prose can have
judgment and rhythm without invented personality. Prefer specific observations
and honest unresolved tension to performative informality.

## Final audit questions

### Scientific integrity

- Can every factual sentence be traced to the packet?
- Are every number and citation correct after the style pass?
- Does each claim state the tested conditions and population?
- Are observation, interpretation, and speculation distinguishable?
- Are negative evidence and limitations close enough to constrain the claim?
- Does any sentence imply a cause, generality, novelty, or superiority that the
  research did not establish?

### Argument and comprehension

- Can a reader state the one to three umbrella claims?
- Does each section answer a question needed for those claims?
- Does each figure or table have an argumentative role?
- Can the reader identify the decisive comparison without guessing?
- Does the paper answer the strongest plausible objection or admit that it
  remains open?
- Do title, abstract, body, and conclusion agree?

### Prose and model-slop risk

- Could the opening belong to an unrelated paper?
- Are significance words standing in for evidence?
- Are transitions carrying information or announcing structure?
- Do paragraph and sentence shapes repeat without a scientific reason?
- Are three-part lists, binary contrasts, and punchline fragments recurring as
  mannerisms?
- Does the text paraphrase itself across sections?
- Did revision preserve technical terms, scope, numbers, and citations?

Read the paper aloud at the end. Repair places where syntax hides the argument,
not every place where the prose sounds formal.
